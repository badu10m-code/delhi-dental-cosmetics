Your child's first dental visit: what happens, and when to book it

By Dr. Animesh Agarwal · 15 Oct 2026 · Kids & Family

Most first visits we see are brought on by a crying child and a swollen face at midnight. It does not have to work that way. A first visit done early, calmly and without any treatment is the single best investment in a child's dental future, because it teaches them the chair is a safe place before anything hurts in it.

When should the first visit be?

By the first birthday, or within six months of the first tooth appearing, whichever comes first. That surprises most parents, who assume dental visits start at school age. The early visits are short: a look, a count of the teeth, a conversation with the parents about brushing and bottles, and maybe a fluoride varnish. The child leaves with a sticker and no memory of pain.

What we do differently with children

A child's appointment runs on their terms. The chair goes up and down a few times first because the lever is interesting. Instruments get introduced as characters that count their teeth. Nothing invasive happens at visit one, even if we can see a cavity. Trust is built in the first visit and spent in a later one, when a small filling might be needed.

The cavities that parents miss

Early childhood decay starts on the back of the upper front teeth, where the milk bottle or the night feed sits against them. It shows up first as a dull white patch near the gumline. By the time a parent notices a brown hole, the decay is months old. If your child sleeps with a bottle of milk or juice, the check-up matters more, not less.

What parents should do at home

  • Brush twice daily with a rice-grain amount of fluoride paste until age three, a pea-size after.
  • Brush for them until they have the handwriting skill to brush well themselves, around six to seven.
  • Keep sweets to mealtimes, not as all-day grazing. Frequency of sugar matters more than quantity.
  • No bottle in bed. Water after the last feed.
  • First check-up by age one, then every six months.

The questions we get most

"Are milk teeth even worth treating, they fall out anyway?" They fall out on a schedule that runs until age twelve, and a decayed milk tooth does not respect that schedule. An infected milk tooth can damage the permanent tooth growing right under it, and an early extraction can shift the space that the adult tooth needs. Milk teeth are placeholders. Protecting the placeholder protects the permanent tooth.

A little one with new teeth? Book their first visit. It takes twenty minutes and buys years of easier dentistry.
