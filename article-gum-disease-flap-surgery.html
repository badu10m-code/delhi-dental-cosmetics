Gum disease and flap surgery: when a deep cleaning is not enough

By Dr. Animesh Agarwal · 15 Oct 2026 · Gum Health

"Doctor, my gums bleed a little when I brush. Is that serious?" It is the question we hear every week, and the honest answer is that bleeding gums are never normal. They are the first letter of a story that, if ignored, ends with loose teeth.

Gum disease moves in stages

Gum disease rarely announces itself with pain. It walks through stages, each quieter than the damage it is doing:

StageWhat is happeningWhat you notice
Healthy gumsFirm, pink, shallow pockets around teethNothing. This is the goal
GingivitisBacteria at the gumline cause inflammationBleeding while brushing, mild redness
Early periodontitisInfection slips below the gumlineBad breath, gums starting to recede
Advanced periodontitisDeep pockets form, bone starts dissolvingLoose teeth, pus, gaps appearing

The uncomfortable truth is in the middle rows: by the time a tooth feels loose, a large part of the bone holding it has already been lost. Bone does not grow back on request.

Why deep cleaning sometimes is not enough

Scaling and root planing, the deep cleaning, works when pockets are shallow enough for instruments to reach the bottom. Beyond a depth of about five millimetres, the tools cannot fully clean the root surface, and the bacteria left behind quietly restart the process. Deep pockets are like a pocket in a coat: whatever falls in, stays in.

What flap surgery actually is

In flap surgery, the gum is gently lifted away from the tooth, like opening a book cover, so the infected root surface and bone become visible and reachable. The dentist removes the hardened deposits, cleans the root, trims any diseased tissue, and sometimes adds bone graft material where the bone has been lost. The gum is then stitched back at a level that is easier to keep clean.

It sounds more dramatic than it feels. It is done under local anaesthesia, and most patients return to work the next day with mild soreness that settles within a week.

What happens if you skip it

An untreated deep pocket is not static. The infection keeps dissolving bone around the tooth until the tooth loosens and comes out, often taking valuable bone with it that a future implant would have needed. Treating gum disease on time is also treating your future options.

After the surgery

The surgery is half the treatment. The other half is maintenance: meticulous brushing, cleaning between the teeth, and professional cleaning visits every three to six months. Patients who keep these visits keep their teeth. Patients who disappear after the surgery usually come back a few years later with the same pockets, deeper.

Gums bleeding when you brush? Book a gum check-up. A ten-minute examination with an X-ray tells you exactly which stage you are at, and what it will take to stop it there.
